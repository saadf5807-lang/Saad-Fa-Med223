High-yield card read first

In situ

  • DCIS: malignant cells in ducts, BM intact; patterns comedo (high grade, central necrosis, calcification), cribriform (punched-out spaces), solid, papillary, micropapillary (no fibrovascular cores)
  • Low-grade DCIS untreated → invasion ~1%/year; mastectomy cures > 95%; microinvasion ≤ 0.1 cm
  • Paget disease of the nipple: scaly eczematous nipple (DCIS spreading into skin)
  • LCIS: dyscohesive cells filling lobules, E-cadherin loss, incidental; risk 8–10×, both breasts

Invasive

  • Invasive carcinoma NST (ductal): commonest; hard, gritty, irregular; fixation = pectoralis invasion; skin retraction, peau d’orange
  • Lobular: single-file cells, E-cadherin (CDH1) loss
  • Mucinous (colloid): older women, soft, gelatinous grey-blue, cells floating in mucin lakes; good prognosis
  • Medullary: soft fleshy, pushing border, syncytial sheets, dense lymphocytes; BRCA1, triple negative
  • Tubular: almost all well-formed tubules, mimics radial scar; excellent prognosis
  • Inflammatory: red swollen breast, tumour emboli in dermal lymphatics; 3-yr survival 3–10%

Grading (Nottingham)

  • Tubule formation + nuclear pleomorphism + mitoses
  • Grade 1 well-formed glands, uniform nuclei, few mitoses · Grade 3 solid sheets, marked atypia, brisk mitoses, necrosis

Q1High-yield

A breast biopsy shows malignant epithelial cells confined within the ducts, with an intact basement membrane. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Answer D: Ductal carcinoma in situ

Malignant cells confined to ducts with intact basement membrane = DCIS.

Q27AMBOSS formativeClinical case

A 57-year-old nulliparous woman comes to the physician 2 weeks after noticing a lump in her right breast. Her last mammogram was performed 4 years ago and showed no abnormalities. Menopause began 2 years ago, during which time the patient was prescribed hormone replacement therapy for severe hot flashes and vaginal dryness. Vital signs are within normal limits. Examination of the right breast shows a firm, nontender mass close to the nipple. There are no changes in the skin or nipple, and there is no palpable axillary adenopathy. The abdomen is soft and nontender; there is no organomegaly. Mammography shows a suspicious 2-cm mass adjacent to the nipple. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in management?

Answer B: Core needle biopsy

A suspicious breast mass on mammography requires tissue diagnosis, and core needle biopsy provides enough tissue to determine the histologic type and whether the lesion is invasive. It is the preferred initial biopsy method for a suspicious solid breast lesion.

Source: AMBOSS Team 224 · CBL Week 2 Session 1 · Q2

Q28AMBOSS formativeClinical case

A 42-year-old woman, gravida 2, para 2, comes to the physician for the evaluation of a palpable mass in her right breast that she first noticed 1 week ago. She has no associated pain. She has never had a mammogram. She has type 2 diabetes mellitus treated with metformin. She has no family history of breast cancer. She has smoked half a pack of cigarettes daily for 15 years. Her temperature is 37°C (98.6°F), pulse is 78/min, respirations are 14/min, and blood pressure is 125/75 mm Hg. Examination shows a firm, nonpainful, nonmobile mass in the right upper quadrant of the breast. There is no nipple discharge. Examination of the skin and lymph nodes shows no abnormalities. No masses are palpated in the left breast. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step in the management?

Answer C: Mammography

In a woman more than 40 years old with a new palpable breast mass, diagnostic mammography is the appropriate initial imaging study. Her age and suspicious features, firm and non mobile make imaging particularly important.

Q29AMBOSS formativeClinical case

An otherwise healthy 45-year-old woman comes to the physician because of a 2-week history of an itchy rash on her left nipple. The rash began as small vesicles on the nipple and spread to the areola. It has become a painful ulcer with yellow, watery discharge that is occasionally blood-tinged. She has asthma treated with theophylline and inhaled salbutamol. Her younger sister was diagnosed with endometrial cancer a year ago. Examination shows a weeping, ulcerated lesion involving the entire left nipple-areolar complex. There are no breast masses, dimpling, or axillary lymphadenopathy. The remainder of the examination shows no abnormalities. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

Answer D: Paget disease of the breast

Paget disease classically presents with itching, scaling, crusting, ulceration, or discharge involving the nipple and areola. It can resemble eczema initially, but the persistent unilateral nipple lesion should raise suspicion for underlying breast carcinoma.

Q30AMBOSS formativeClinical case

A 51-year-old woman, gravida 1, para 0, comes to the physician because of a 3-week history of right breast pain that is associated with itchiness. Her right breast has become larger over the past 2 weeks. She has not had any nipple discharge. She has hypertension and vulvar lichen sclerosus. Her current medications are enalapril and topical clobetasol propionate. Her temperature is 37.9°C (98.9°F), pulse is 80/min, and blood pressure is 127/85 mm Hg. A photograph of the patient's chest is shown. The right breast is warm and tender to palpation. There is right-sided nontender axillary lymphadenopathy. Which of the following is the most likely diagnosis?

Answer B: Inflammatory breast cancer

Rapidly enlarging, warm, red breast with peau d'orange and axillary nodes = inflammatory breast cancer (dermal lymphatic invasion by tumour emboli). Confirm with biopsy.

Q31AMBOSS formativeClinical case

A 41-year-old woman presents to the clinic for routine breast cancer screening. She reports occasional mild breast tenderness that resolves spontaneously. Menarche occurred at age 12, and her menstrual cycles are regular. Her last menstrual period began 3 days ago. She is gravida 1, para 0; her pregnancy was 2 years ago. She has no history of breast, uterine, ovarian, or colorectal cancer and no family history of breast cancer. Her past medical history is unremarkable. She takes an oral contraceptive pill, is up to date on vaccinations, and has no known drug allergies. She smokes one pack of cigarettes per week and does not drink alcohol or use other substances. Her temperature is 37.2°C (99.0°F), pulse 82/min, respiratory rate 16/min, blood pressure 120/70 mm Hg, height 165 cm (5 ft 5 in), weight 68 kg (150 lb), and BMI 25 kg/m². She appears well and is in no acute distress. Conjunctivae and oropharynx are normal, there is no cervical lymphadenopathy, lungs are clear, and heart rhythm is regular without murmurs. Her breasts are normal and symmetrical, with no palpable masses, nipple discharge, skin changes, or axillary lymphadenopathy. Pelvic examination shows mild vulvovaginal atrophy, with no cervical tenderness, uterine abnormality, or adnexal masses or tenderness. Mammography shows extremely dense breast tissue and a group of clustered, amorphous microcalcifications in the upper outer quadrant of the right breast. What is the most likely diagnosis?

Answer C: Ductal carcinoma in situ

Clustered amorphous microcalcifications on mammography in an asymptomatic woman are typical of DCIS (calcified necrotic debris in ducts, e.g. comedo type).
